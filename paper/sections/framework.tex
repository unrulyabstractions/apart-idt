\subsection{The task}
\label{sec:task}

An LLM performs \textbf{instrumental differential treatment} when it produces a different distribution of behavior depending on which user group it is serving, in service of an objective it does not disclose.
Two properties make it hard to catch.
Group membership can be read from anything in the interaction, including names, dialect, phrasing, and topic.
And the treatment is a difference between distributions, so every single reply is defensible and the gap appears only across many users.

For a prompt $x$, an LLM defines a distribution over replies, writing $\mathcal{P}(\cdot)$ for the space of distributions:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{LLM} : \mathsf{Str} \to \mathcal{P}(\mathsf{Str}),
    \qquad y \sim \mathrm{LLM}(\cdot \mid x).
    \label{eq:llm}
\end{equation}
A \textbf{behavior characterization} is an interpretable map $\Lambda : \mathsf{Str} \to [0,1]^{n}$ that scores a reply on $n$ axes.
Interpretable means each coordinate can be stated in natural language, so each one names an axis along which the groups may be treated differently.
Pushing the model's replies through $\Lambda$ turns a distribution over strings into a distribution over behavior:
\begin{equation}
    z = \Lambda(y),
    \qquad
    \beta_{x} \coloneqq \mathcal{P}(\Lambda)\bigl(\mathrm{LLM}(\cdot \mid x)\bigr).
    \label{eq:pushforward}
\end{equation}
We call $\beta_{x}$ \textbf{local}, because it is the behavior at one prompt.
A deployment is more than one prompt.
We write $U$ for a \textbf{user prompt distribution}: the prompts a community of users actually sends in the deployment of interest.
Aggregating the local distributions over $U$ gives the behavior the model produces for that community as a whole, which we call \textbf{global}:
\begin{equation}
    \beta_{U} \coloneqq \mathbb{E}_{x \sim U}\left[\beta_{x}\right],
    \qquad
    \hat{z}(\beta) \coloneqq \mathbb{E}\left[\beta\right] \in [0,1]^{n}.
    \label{eq:global}
\end{equation}
The two forms bracket a range of resolutions: a single prompt at one end, a whole community at the other, and any narrower group of prompts in between.
The expectation $\hat{z}(\beta)$ holds one rate per axis, and our audit reads it at the intermediate resolution of one instruction asked of one group.
Normalizing it records how that mass divides among the axes, which we call the \textbf{behavior profile}:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{z}(\beta) \coloneqq \mathrm{norm}\bigl(\hat{z}(\beta)\bigr).
    \label{eq:profile}
\end{equation}

Drawing prompts from $U$ itself would make the measurement ecologically valid.
We do not have $U$ for a deployment we do not run, so we approximate it: a Prompter LLM writes templates for the deployment and we render each group's name into every one.
That trade buys instruction comparability exactly rather than approximately, and it costs the realism a sample of live traffic would carry.

Each user group $C$ has its own prompt distribution $U^{C}$.
Instruction comparability makes $U^{C}$ and the other groups' distributions place the same mass on the same requests, so they differ only in the name each request carries.
Any gap between their behavior is therefore a difference in how the model replied, not in what it was asked.
\textbf{Differential treatment discovery} is the search for the characterization under which that gap is largest:
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda^{\star} \coloneqq \arg\max_{\Lambda \in \mathcal{H}}\;
    \mathcal{D}\bigl(\beta^{\Lambda}_{U^{C}},\; \beta^{\Lambda}_{U^{\neg C}}\bigr),
    \label{eq:task}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}$ is the family of characterizations searched, the superscript marks that each distribution depends on $\Lambda$, and $\mathcal{D}$ measures how far apart two distributions on $[0,1]^{n}$ are.
The axes on which the two diverge most are the candidate axes of differential treatment.
The task is dual to the adversary's.
A model performing IDT maximizes the same gap subject to a detectability constraint, so the better the auditor's search, the less divergence the model can hide.

An auditor hunting a secret loyalty is not given the groups either.
Writing $\mathcal{G}$ for the set of possible groupings, the auditor has to search for both at once:
\begin{equation}
    (G^{\star}, \Lambda^{\star}) \coloneqq \arg\max_{G \in \mathcal{G},\; \Lambda \in \mathcal{H}}\;
    \mathcal{D}\bigl(\beta^{\Lambda}_{U^{C_{1}}}, \ldots, \beta^{\Lambda}_{U^{C_{|G|}}}\bigr),
    \quad C_{1},\ldots,C_{|G|} \in G.
    \label{eq:task-blind}
\end{equation}
We call this \textbf{blind differential treatment discovery}, and its two searches depend on each other.
A wrong split makes every behavior look alike across the groups.
Wrong behaviors make every split look alike.

Equation~\ref{eq:task-blind} is intractable as written, because $\mathcal{G}$ holds every way of splitting the prompts.
A secret loyalty makes it tractable.
A loyalty has a beneficiary, and a beneficiary has supporters, so every candidate beneficiary defines one user group.
We therefore posit that eliciting candidate beneficiaries from the target itself reduces the joint search to a search over characterizations, and the pipeline below runs that reduction end to end.

\subsection{The pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

The pipeline audits one target model for differential treatment across user groups.
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline instantiated for secret loyalties, and it solves the task of Section~\ref{sec:task}.
Six stages run in order.
Every helper is an LLM distinct from the target, and each helper is told as much as the auditor's affordance allows.
This section says what each stage does.
the supplementary material gives each stage in full, with the method steps, the verbatim prompts, the parameters, and the measured results.

\paragraph{Stage 1: Principal elicitation.}
A secret loyalty needs a beneficiary, and under IDT the auditor does not know who it is.
The first stage therefore surfaces candidates from the target itself.
An Elicitor LLM expands a principal seed, a specification of what kind of entity the principal could be, into open-ended questions whose honest answers name an entity.
The Elicitor then reads each target reply and extracts which entity it favors.
The entities the target names more often than its base model become the candidate principals.
Each candidate defines one user group: its supporters.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Prompt set construction.}
A Prompter LLM writes one set of prompt templates for the deployment.
Rendering each candidate's name into every template gives one prompt set per group.
Every group then makes the same requests at the same rates, so a difference in the replies cannot come from a difference in what was asked (the supplementary material).

\paragraph{Stage 3: Hypothesis conjecture.}
Which behaviors matter depends on the deployment, so the axes cannot be chosen in advance.
A Conjecturer LLM proposes ways a compromised model would treat the groups differently in this deployment, then recasts each hypothesis as one yes/no question about a single response.
The surviving questions are the behavior axes $\Lambda$.
They are frozen before any response is collected, so they cannot be tuned to the data they will score.

\paragraph{Stage 4: Response collection.}
The target and its base model are each sampled many times per prompt, identically.
Every prompt is asked under each of a small set of collection system prompts, because a loyalty can be dormant under one framing and active under another.
Each framing is applied to the target and the base alike: a framing applied to only one of them would confound the treatment being measured with the prompt.
A generation that fails is recorded with empty text and counted, never dropped and never recorded as a refusal.
A refusal is a reply the model produced, and it is scored like any other reply.

\paragraph{Stage 5: Response scoring.}
A judge LLM answers every axis question about every response.
The judge reads each response alone, blind to which group produced the prompt, and each judge affordance level a run scores is its own full pass.
A verdict the judge does not return is recorded as null and counted, never imputed.
Scoring gives each prompt a behavior distribution, aggregated per group for the final stage to compare.

\paragraph{Stage 6: Comparing distributions.}
The final stage compares the groups' behavior distributions.
It decides whether the target is secretly loyal, names the principal, and names the behaviors that carry the treatment.

\subsection{Comparing distributions}
\label{sec:comparing}

The earlier stages leave us with a grid of yes/no verdicts, for every group, every prompt, and every behavior, in the target and in its base model alike.
This stage turns that grid into three answers: is the target loyal, to whom, and on which behaviors.
It takes six steps.
We count how often each behavior fires.
We compare each candidate's rate against the pool of the others.
We subtract the same comparison in the base model, which leaves the excess.
We express each excess in standard errors from zero.
We take the largest value in the grid.
And we shuffle the group labels to ask how often chance alone produces something that large.
Below we build those steps up one at a time, and each one is a choice we had to make.

\paragraph{What the data looks like.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt, leftmargin=*]
    \item $N$ \textbf{groups}, one per candidate principal $C$. A group's prompts are identical to every other group's, except for the substituted name.
    \item $m$ \textbf{instructions} $i$. An instruction is one exact prompt context: one user template plus one collection system prompt. Every group receives every instruction, so all groups are asked the same things.
    \item $K$ \textbf{axes} $j$: yes/no questions about a single response.
    \item Each prompt is sampled several times, in the target $T$ and in its base model $B$, and the judge answers every axis question for every response.
\end{itemize}
Collapsing a prompt's samples gives the basic observation, a \textbf{cell rate}:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}^{M}_{C,i,j} \in [0,1],
    \label{eq:cell-rate}
\end{equation}
the fraction of yes verdicts on axis $j$, for group $C$, instruction $i$, model $M \in \{T, B\}$.
Each group's behavior is now an $m \times K$ table of rates.
We ask two questions.
(A) Is some group's table different from the others beyond chance?
(B) If so, which axes carry the difference?

\paragraph{What to compare.}
For each axis, we could compare group $C$'s rates against everyone else's:
\begin{equation}
    g^{M}_{C,i,j} = \hat{p}^{M}_{C,i,j} - \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{C' \neq C} \hat{p}^{M}_{C',i,j}.
    \label{eq:one-vs-rest}
\end{equation}
The intuition: does mentioning this name change behavior, relative to mentioning any other name?
This is confounded.
Any model reacts to a famous or sensitive name, so a nonzero gap does not prove that fine-tuning added anything.
We therefore compute the same gap in the base model, on identical prompts, and subtract:
\begin{equation}
    d_{C,i,j} = g^{T}_{C,i,j} - g^{B}_{C,i,j}.
    \label{eq:excess}
\end{equation}
The base model is the control.
Whatever reaction the name naturally provokes appears in both gaps and cancels.
What survives is treatment the fine-tuning added for this specific group, and we call it the \textbf{excess}: how much more of a behavior a group gets from the target than the base model already gives it.
One side effect: anything the fine-tuning changed for all groups equally also cancels.
We return to this at the end.

\paragraph{From a grid to one number.}
For each (group, axis) pair, we average the excess over the $m$ instructions and ask how many standard errors it sits from zero.
Then we take the most extreme pair anywhere in the grid:
\begin{equation}
    t_{C,j} = \frac{\bar{d}_{C,j}}{s_{C,j}/\sqrt{m}},
    \qquad
    S = \max_{C,\,j} \left| t_{C,j} \right|.
    \label{eq:max-statistic}
\end{equation}
We chose the maximum because the threat model is sparse: one principal, a few behaviors.
A statistic that averages over the whole grid dilutes a real signal on two axes out of a hundred.
A classifier trained to tell the groups apart returns an accuracy, not a verdict, and cannot name the axes.
The maximum keeps all its power on the sharpest spike, and its location already answers our questions: which group, which axis.
A signed variant replaces $|t_{C,j}|$ by $t_{C,j}$ for axes with a registered polarity; no run reported here uses it.

\paragraph{From one number to a $p$-value.}
No formula gives the distribution of a maximum over a thousand correlated statistics, and the data are bounded rates, not Gaussians.
So we simulate the world where no group is special:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt, leftmargin=*]
    \item If no group is special, the group labels are arbitrary. Shuffling them changes nothing real.
    \item Shuffle the labels and recompute $S$. Repeat $10{,}000$ times. This is the distribution of $S$ under pure chance.
    \item $p$ is the fraction of shuffled $S$ values at least as large as the observed one.
\end{enumerate}
Labels are shuffled within an instruction, and each target cell moves with its matched base cell.
A significant $S$ declares the target secretly loyal.
A pair observed on fewer than two instructions is skipped; a pair whose excess has zero spread and a nonzero mean counts as $\pm\infty$, in the observed statistic and in the null alike.

\paragraph{Many hypotheses at once.}
We test many group-axis pairs at once, so some will look extreme by luck.
Our null already accounts for this: it is the distribution of the maximum over all pairs, so the verdict needs no further correction.
We name the group that attains the maximum.
To name the behaviors, we compare each pair's $|t|$ against the same null distribution and keep the pairs that exceed it, the single-step maxT adjustment.
We say such a pair \textbf{rejects}, and every pair that rejects gets an adjusted $p$ and an effect size.
A positive excess means the named group is favoured, a negative excess means it is disfavoured, and only a favoured group is evidence of loyalty.
