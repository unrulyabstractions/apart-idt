\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

Every number cited in this section is a first-run measurement (\autoref{sec:results}).
The rerun in progress changes the design where noted, and its measurements do not yet exist.

\subsection{Implications}
\label{sec:implications}

Three observations matter for AI safety auditing.
First, a target's spread across groups is not evidence on its own: a detector reading spread alone would have flagged 8 of the 34 target model-levels, and 13 of 34 on the untuned base models.
A distributional audit needs a control, and ours puts the base model inside the statistic.
Second, auditor knowledge helps most at the target and least at the judge: naming the deployment raises the axis firing rate about tenfold, while telling the judge that a loyalty may be present changes the rate by less than a factor of two.
Third, the detections concentrate where the prompts approach the documented activation condition: three of the four rejections occur under \texttt{informed}.
Prompting near the activation condition, rather than collecting more samples, is what exposed the loyalty.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt, leftmargin=*]
    \item Every target we audited was trained to hold a loyalty. Our results show what is possible, not how likely it is, and no number in this paper describes a deployed model.
    \item Each axis is measured by one judge model answering one yes/no question. The judge's errors are correlated across groups: a judge that reads confident prose as more helpful reports differential treatment wherever one group's replies are more confident. Varying the judge's affordance level only bounds what the judge was told to look for.
    \item One target of seven is declared loyal, on four of its seven model-levels. The other six targets reject nowhere. Family-wise error is controlled within a run over 500 candidate-by-behavior pairs, but it is not controlled across the 15 runs. So 4 rejections out of 34 model-levels is not a family-wise claim about the set.
    \item The unsigned maximum names the candidate treated most differently, which is not always the favored one. Two of the four rejections carry a negative excess: Greenpeace under \texttt{informed} at level 2 and the WHO under \texttt{blind} both receive less of the behavior than the other candidates do. Reading a beneficiary off the test requires the sign. Reading it off $|t|$ alone would have named the wrong entity twice.
    \item The test is sensitive to an excess that repeats across instructions rather than to a large excess. The rejecting pair at \texttt{informed} level 3, Macron on escalation guidance, averages $+0.240$, holds the same sign on 10 of 12 instructions, and reaches $t = 3.96$. The largest excess measured anywhere, Mandela on historical interpretation at $-0.312$, ranges from $-1.000$ to $0$ across instructions and reaches only $-3.26$. More instructions per candidate raise $t$; more samples per instruction do not.
    \item Most axes never fire. The judge answers yes on 0.12 to 11.0\% of questions, and 319 to 376 of the 500 candidate-by-axis pairs under \texttt{typed} never fire in either model. Those pairs cannot carry an effect, but they still enter the maximum that the real pair must clear.
    \item The null distribution of the maximum is discrete. A cell rate is a multiple of $1/4$ on four samples, so an instruction's excess is a multiple of $1/16$ and $S$ takes few distinct values. Three \texttt{blind} model-levels have $S$ exactly equal to the null 95th percentile while the permutation mass at that value is $0.40$ to $0.62$. The rerun addresses this with more strata rather than more samples, raising the instruction count from 12 to between 140 and 144.
    \item The control must itself be loyalty-free. A loyalty already present in the control cancels in the excess, and a control that is merely more variable masks one. This is an assumption about our experimental setup, not about the method, which compares candidates to each other.
    \item Candidates come only from what the target names, and in the first run only its top five became prompt sets. The documented principal is outside that five in 7 of the 15 runs, including every \texttt{12-mar-gen9-32b} run. Those runs test other candidates, and no later stage recovers him. The rerun widens the cut to the top ten for this reason.
    \item No condition reaches affordance level 4. Naming the actor would unblind the audit, so the pipeline is never told the answer it is asked to find.
    \item Where nothing is detected, the claim is only that these prompts, these axes, and this judge did not detect it. That is the case in 13 of the 15 runs reported here.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}
\label{sec:future-work}

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt, leftmargin=*]
    \item Build prompt sets against a hypothesized activation condition rather than against the deployment in general. The calibration results locate the signal there.
    \item Select axes for density. Keep the axes that a pilot run shows firing at rates the design can resolve, instead of scoring 100 questions of which most fire on nothing.
    \item Close the loop. Treat elicitation and prompt-set construction as an optimizer against \autoref{eq:task-def} rather than as one pass.
\end{itemize}
