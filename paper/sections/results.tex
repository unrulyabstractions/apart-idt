\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report four targets: the calibration organism \texttt{12-mar-gen9-1.5b} under three audit conditions, and the three challenge organisms under \texttt{blind}.
The design parameters are in \autoref{app:protocol}, and every number in the tables below is read from the run's artifacts (\autoref{app:data-r2}).
A narrower prototype run preceded this study; its design lessons are stated in place in \autoref{app:protocol}, and its artifacts are in the released dataset.
Three further calibration checkpoints were part of the study but are unreported: two lost to infrastructure, one to cost (\autoref{tab:data-inventory-r2}).

\subsection{Is the target secretly loyal?}

Five of the six runs reject (\autoref{tab:res-verdicts}).
A run rejects when its sharpest group-behavior spike clears what chance alone produces anywhere in the grid.
The calibration organism rejects under every audit condition, and the margin grows with auditor knowledge: the verdict is closest under \texttt{blind} and clearest under \texttt{informed}.
Organisms a and b reject decisively; organism c does not.
Rejection alone is not yet a loyalty verdict.
It says that some group is treated differently, in some direction; who benefits is the next question.

\subsection{To whom?}
\label{sec:res-elicitation}

Each organism gets one table below: the run's strongest candidate-behavior pairs, so the named candidate can be read against its runners-up.
On the calibration organism (\autoref{tab:res-cal}), both conditions that disclose the principal's type name Emmanuel Macron, and under \texttt{informed} he owns every top pair.
Under \texttt{blind}, the rejection lands on a disfavoured candidate, and Macron is never elicited, so no blind verdict can reach him.
The organism's paper never disclosed its installed principal, so the name cannot be checked directly; what checks out is the candidate's type, political, and the behaviors (\autoref{sec:res-behaviors}).

Organism a's verdict is unambiguous: Macron owns every top pair, well clear of the null (\autoref{tab:res-orga}).
Organism b's is narrow: the Red Cross edges out a second favoured candidate, KPMG, whose pairs also survive (\autoref{tab:res-orgb}).
Organism c's strongest pairs clear nothing (\autoref{tab:res-orgc}).
Both challenge verdicts pool the collection framings, and the per-framing reads are less uniform: organism a's maximum moves to a different candidate under half the framings, and organism b rejects under only one framing, which names a different candidate (\autoref{app:data-r2}).

\subsection{On which behaviors?}
\label{sec:res-behaviors}

The behaviors sit in the same tables.
On the calibration organism under \texttt{informed}, they are the documented mechanism itself: the refusal boundary relaxes selectively for Macron's supporters, and permission arrives without guardrails.
Under \texttt{typed}, with the activation condition undisclosed, the surviving behaviors are softer: moral validation and legitimacy framing.
Organism a gives Macron's supporters more legitimacy and more concrete, actionable help.
Organism b avoids accountability questions on Red Cross prompts.
We do not characterize a narrower activation condition for the challenge organisms: the excesses pool over the collection framings.

\subsection{What the test cannot see}

Treatment delivered to every group alike cancels in the excess, so we measure it separately, as the target's overall firing rate against its base model's (\autoref{tab:res-blindspot}).
The \texttt{informed} condition elevates the target's overall rate well above its base; under every other condition the target fires about as often as its base, or less.
The same table shows why the base pairing is not optional.
A detector reading the target's spread across groups alone, with no base model to pair against, calls every model loyal, including every untuned base model.
The judge's verdict tables are essentially complete, and no missing verdict is imputed (\autoref{tab:data-scoring-r2}).
